# Asymmetric Activations Keep Sinking Preactivations

Subtitle candidate: A Mechanism of Plasticity Loss across MLPs, CNNs and Transformers, with the kunekune Activation

<!-- 日本語見出し: ## 要旨 -->
## Abstract

Preactivation sinking, response reduction, and linearization in continual learning are known phenomena. We connect them through a single mechanism. Each time a task is learned, the readout aligns with the current task. This alignment pushes preactivations downward at the next switch. The input-mean and bias pathways, together with upstream pathways that move with learning, carry the push to the position. Asymmetric activations sink deeply. Growth of the width increases this depth. Units that have sunk lose their response. This reduces relearning. For a linear readout with cross-entropy, we derive a condition for positive alignment. In a fixed-feature model, we show that the push at the switch is proportional to the product of the activation value and its slope. We verified each link in this chain through interventions or analyses of saved states in three architectures: MLPs, CNNs, and a 2-block character GPT. Blocking the input-mean and bias pathways preserved relearning (200 tasks in MLPs and 30 tasks in CNNs). Limiting the width prevented collapse (growth caps for random-label ELU and Transformers). Preserving weight rotation restored relearning (row-norm projection applied only to the FFN one step before the readout in the Transformer). Response-field exchange with matched initial activities and predictions changed relearning in both directions in all three architectures (partially in the Transformer). The only aspects that changed across architectures were the identity of the carrier (from a fixed input mean to a common write from upstream) and the form of the harm caused by width (from a deeper position to the freezing of rotation). From this mechanism, we derive kunekune, an activation that matches its curvature scale to the width to preserve response. In real-label MLPs, it preserved relearning at least as well as existing countermeasures. In CNNs, it outperformed ELU, which lost response as the width grew, when the number of updates per task was extended.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ## 1 序論 -->
## 1 Introduction

Plasticity loss, in which continued training impairs relearning, has been associated with unit closure, weight growth, and reduced representation rank [Dohare 2024]. As the preactivation distribution shifts, units close or become nearly linear and lose response. In ReLU networks immediately after a task switch, preactivations were also observed to be pushed toward the negative side, causing units to die [Lyle 2025]. Another account describes preactivations being pushed into the tails of the activation, where gradients shut down [Lillo & Cheney 2026]. In a ReLU network at initialization, random readout weights yield a positive expected gradient with respect to a positive preactivation. Gradient descent therefore acts to shrink that preactivation [Li 2023; Shvetsov 2026]. Formal proofs are limited to ReLU. Similar drift in GELU and SiLU was reported experimentally [Shvetsov 2026]. We ask why this push is rebuilt at every task switch, which pathways carry it to the position, and what function is lost to reduce relearning.

The answer is a four-stage mechanism. (i) Learning a task aligns the features and the readout with the labels of the current task. At the next switch, this alignment creates a push proportional to the product of the activation value and its slope. (ii) The input-mean and bias pathways carry the push to the position. In layers whose upstream changes with learning, they also carry that displacement. (iii) Asymmetric activations sink deeply under the push. Growth of the width increases the depth. (iv) Units that have sunk lose their response. This reduces relearning. Blocking the pathways or preserving the response preserves relearning. We do not claim that all units always sink. We state the conditions under which each stage holds.

Our verification has three pillars: interventions on the position pathways, bidirectional interventions on the response, and rescue and non-rescue by growth caps. We establish a reference in MLPs (Section 4). We verify the same four stages in CNNs (Section 5) and a 2-block character GPT (Section 6). The aspects that changed across architectures were the identity of the carrier and the form of the harm caused by width. Both are part of the stated mechanism. From this mechanism, we derive kunekune, an activation that matches its curvature scale to the width to preserve response (Section 7). We compare it with existing countermeasures on real-label tasks.

Relation to prior work. In a ReLU network at initialization, a random readout yields a positive expected gradient with respect to a positive preactivation [Li 2023; Shvetsov 2026]. Li et al. state that their theorem does not apply after learning begins. Experiments with purely random labels in a one-hidden-layer ReLU network showed that the bias drifted toward the negative side and ReLU units died [Cornacchia 2021]. A decrease in preactivations for many inputs immediately after a switch was also reported as an observation in ReLU networks [Lyle 2025]. We add that the learned readout reloads the push at every switch. We identify the carriers as the input-mean, bias, and upstream pathways. We identify response as the function lost and link this loss to width growth. We verify these claims through interventions and analyses of saved states in three architectures. Section 6 discusses the relation to normalization and effective learning rates [Lyle 2024; Kosson 2024]. Section 7 discusses the relation to activation design [Lillo & Cheney 2026] and to weight shrinkage, regularization, and unit reinitialization [Ash & Adams 2020; Kumar 2025; Dohare 2024; Sokar 2023].

## 2 Setup and measurements

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 2.1 学び直しの評価 -->
### 2.1 Evaluating relearning

We define plasticity loss as a decline in the ability to learn later tasks under conditions in which the network could learn initially. Tasks are learned sequentially. The weights and optimizer state are carried over at each task switch. We measure adaptation to new tasks, not retention of past tasks.

For image classification, we use online accuracy, the minibatch accuracy before each update averaged within a task. Even if two networks reach the same end-of-task accuracy, slower adaptation along the way yields lower online accuracy. For real-label tasks whose difficulty itself changes, we compare with a fresh network trained on the same task (Appendix A). The three-phase metrics for character GPT are defined in Section 6.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 2.2 位置、幅、応答 -->
### 2.2 Position, width, and response

Let the input to a layer be $x$ and let $\mu$ be the mean of the input distribution used for evaluation. We decompose the preactivation of unit $i$ as $z_i(x)=w_i^\top x+b_i=m_i+w_i^\top(x-\mu)$, with $m_i=w_i^\top\mu+b_i$. We call $m_i$ the common position, $s_i=\operatorname{sd}_x[z_i(x)]$ the width, and $r_i=m_i/s_i$ the relative position ($s_i>0$). We call movement of the common position toward the negative side sinking. Whereas $m$ represents the raw change in position, $r$ represents position relative to width. Changes in $r$ therefore also include changes in the denominator $s$.

We define the open fraction as $p_i=\Pr_x[z_i(x)>0]$. We define the local response as the derivative used in training, $d_i(x)=\varphi'(z_i(x))$. For MLPs, the death rate is the fraction of units with $\max_x|d_i(x)|<10^{-6}$ over all training images of each task. For CNNs and GPT, death is measured by the open fraction on a fixed evaluation set (probe; thresholds in Appendix A).

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 2.3 課題と構造の役割 -->
### 2.3 Roles of the tasks and architectures

We use one-hidden-layer networks to study the fixed-feature analysis and the formation of the push. We use two-hidden-layer MLPs for interventions on the position pathways and the response. CNNs and GPT test the mechanism in architectures whose upstream changes. Real-label 5+1 CIFAR tests the case of learning new classes sequentially and supports design comparisons. In random-label tasks, a fixed set of 1,200 images from MNIST or CIFAR-10 receives independent random labels from 10 classes at each task (following the setup of [Kumar 2025]). The task design of real-label 5+1 CIFAR also follows Kumar et al. Hard tasks with 5 classes and easy tasks with 1 class from CIFAR-100 alternate without revisiting classes (networks in Appendix A). We evaluate on the hard tasks.

The main MLP experiments use Adam without normalization layers. We call the condition with pixel values divided by 255 raw. We call the condition standardized with per-channel constants std. Each comparison specifies the seeds, branch points, evaluation windows, and aggregation method. Appendix A collects the details.

## 3 Mechanism: the learned state moves the position

**〔3-0〕本節は機構を四つの段に分けて述べる。**

This section states the mechanism in four stages. (i) At every task switch, each unit moves in the direction that suppresses its output, and learning the task is what creates this push. (ii) The input-mean and bias pathways carry the push to the position; when the upstream layers move, their displacement is carried as well. (iii) Asymmetric activations sink deeply, and the growth of the width increases the depth. (iv) A unit that has sunk loses its response. Section 4 verifies the four stages in this order in MLPs, and Sections 5 and 6 carry them to convolutional networks and Transformers.

### 3.1 Learning creates the push at the switch

**〔3.1-1〕切替前の学習は、特徴と読み出しを現課題のラベルに整列させ、切替の押しを作る（装填）。**

Learning before the switch aligns the features and the readout with the labels of the current task, and this alignment creates the push at the switch; we call this loading. Let $\phi_n$ be the features of input $n=1,\ldots,N$, let $y_n$ be the one-hot vector of the old label ($C$ classes), and let the logits be $\ell_n=V\phi_n+b$. Let $V^c$ and $b^c$ denote the readout and the bias centered across classes. We measure the strength of the alignment by the coefficient $\kappa$ of the projection of $V^c$ onto the direction of $F=\sum_n(y_n-\mathbf1/C)\phi_n^\top$. Let $L$ be the mean cross-entropy. When $F\ne0$,

$$
\kappa=\frac{\langle V^c,F\rangle_F}{\|F\|_F^2}
\ge\frac{N(\log C-L)-B}{\|F\|_F^2}
$$

holds (proof in Appendix C). The term $B=\sum_c(n_c-N/C)b^c_c$ comes from the imbalance of the class counts $n_c$ and from the bias, and it vanishes when the classes are balanced. Hence $\kappa>0$ whenever $L<\log C-B/N$; neither independence between the features and the labels nor perfect classification is required.

**〔3.1-2〕この整列が切替で押しになる。一隠れ層の固定特徴模型を考える。**

This alignment becomes the push at the switch. Consider a fixed-feature model with one hidden layer. Fix $\phi_{in}=\varphi(z_i(x_n))$ and $d_{in}=\varphi'(z_i(x_n))$, let the old labels be independent and uniform across inputs, and let the new labels be independent of the network state and uniform as well. Write the readout as $v_{ic}=v^0_{ic}+\kappa\sum_m(y_{mc}-1/C)\phi_{im}$, and keep $\kappa>0$ fixed when taking the expectation over the old labels. We further assume that the correlation between $v^0$ and the old labels averages out, and that the prediction before the switch is in the limit of a confident correct answer, $\pi_n\to y_n$. We call these assumptions H1 (Appendix C).

**〔3.1-3〕層入力を固定し、重みとbiasを同じ学習率 η で一標本SGD更新すると…**

Fix the layer input and update the weights and the bias by one-sample SGD with the same learning rate $\eta$. With $\delta_{in}=\partial\ell_n/\partial z_i(x_n)$, the position moves by $\Delta m_i=-\eta\,\delta_{in}K_n$ with $K_n=\mu^\top x_n+1$ (the meaning of $K_n$ is given in Section 3.2). Define the push that sinks the position, averaged over the new labels, as

$$
S_i=\sum_nK_nd_{in}\langle v_i,\pi_n-\mathbf1/C\rangle
$$

Then the expected displacement of one full-batch step on the mean loss is $-(\eta/N)S_i$, so $S_i>0$ is the sinking direction. Averaging also over the old labels under H1 gives

$$
\mathbb E_{\rm old}S_i
=\kappa(1-1/C)\sum_nK_n\varphi'(z_{in})\varphi(z_{in})
$$

This is because the label correlations between different inputs vanish and only the diagonal terms of the same input remain. We call the sum on the right-hand side, the weighted sum of the product of the value and the slope, the self-form. In this model, the sign of the product of the activation value and its slope sets the direction of the push, and its magnitude is proportional to the alignment $\kappa$ that learning has created.

### 3.2 The pathways that carry the push to the position

**〔3.2-1〕共通位置を動かすのはbiasだけではない。**

The bias is not the only thing that moves the common position. The weight update that learns the features also changes the common position, through its component along the input mean. In a multilayer network, moreover, the features that enter the layer change themselves. The finite displacement from time $t$ to $t+1$ is exactly

$$
\Delta m_i=(\Delta w_i)^\top\mu_t
+w_{i,t+1}^\top\Delta\mu+\Delta b_i
$$

The first term is the weight pathway, the third term is the bias pathway, and the second term is the pathway of upstream change. This decomposition does not depend on the optimizer. The sign of each term is examined separately.

**〔3.2-2〕§3.1の K_n は、固定入力のSGD一歩で重みの経路とbiasの経路が運ぶ量を合わせたものである。**

The factor $K_n=\mu^\top x_n+1$ of Section 3.1 combines what the weight pathway ($\mu^\top x_n$) and the bias pathway ($+1$) carry in one SGD step with fixed inputs. When every component of the input is non-negative, as for raw images or for ReLU outputs before centering, every $K_n\ge1$, and a positive local gradient lowers the common position. In general, $\mathbb E_xK=\|\mu\|^2+1>0$ does not make every $K_n$ positive, and for standardized inputs $K_n$ can be negative.

**〔3.2-3〕厳密に中心化された固定入力では μ=0、K_n=1 となり…**

For exactly centered fixed inputs, $\mu=0$ and $K_n=1$; the pathway from the weights to the common position disappears, but the bias pathway remains. Whether centering alone can suppress sinking therefore also depends on the remaining action of the bias. In a layer whose upstream moves with learning, the second term $w^\top\Delta\mu$ is added to these. Its magnitude is measured in Sections 5 and 6.

### 3.3 Sign boundaries and width for each activation

**〔3.3-1〕まずReLUでは φ′(z)φ(z)=max(z,0) である。**

For ReLU, $\varphi'(z)\varphi(z)=\max(z,0)$. If $K_n$ is positive for every input, then, regardless of the distribution, the self-form of any fixed state with at least one open input is positive, and no contribution lifts the unit from the negative side.

**〔3.3-2〕他の活性化の比較には、ガウス・一定正 K を仮定する。leaky ReLU の零点。**

To compare the other activations, we assume $z\sim\mathcal N(m,s^2)$ and an input-independent $K_n=\bar K>0$. With $G_s(r)=\mathbb E[\varphi'(z)\varphi(z)]/s$, the sign of the self-form is the sign of $G_s(r)$. For leaky ReLU (negative-side slope $0<a<1$), $G_s$ has a closed form $G(r)$ that does not depend on the width (Appendix C). $G(0)>0$, and there is a unique zero on the negative side. For $a=0.1$, for example, the zero is at $r\simeq-1.72$, where the open fraction is about 4%. Even in a state where positive and negative inputs are equally frequent, the product on the negative side is only $a^2$ times that of a positive input of the same magnitude, so the sinking direction remains. The zero is the sign boundary of the self-form, not a stopping point of the training trajectory.

**〔3.3-3〕ELUでは 0<α≤1 のもとで…SiLUとGELUには点ごとの不等式が成り立つ。**

For ELU with $0<\alpha\le1$, this Gaussian, constant-positive-$K$ self-form is positive for $m\ge0$ and has a unique zero on the negative side. For SiLU and GELU, the pointwise inequality $\varphi(z)\varphi'(z)\ge z/4$ holds regardless of the distribution, and it is strict for $z\ne0$ (proof in Appendix C). Hence, under constant positive $K$, the self-form is positive whenever $m\ge0$ and $z$ is not identically zero.

**〔3.3-4〕自己形が弱くなった後の相2として、凍結・線形化した網を二乗誤差とridgeで当てはめる模型を考える。**

As phase 2, after the self-form has weakened, we consider a model that fits a frozen, linearized network by squared error with ridge regularization. Under the conditions of Appendix C, the quantity that sets the sign of the push changes from $\mathbb E[\varphi'\varphi]$ to $\operatorname{Cov}(\varphi',\varphi)$; for leaky ReLU, if inputs lie on both the positive and the negative side, the push is on the sinking side even deeper in the negative region than the zero of the self-form. The additional condition (H4) that ties the phase-2 push to the actual arrangement of inputs has been checked against real states only for MLPs (Appendices C and D).

**〔3.3-5〕最後に、同じガウス・一定正 K の模型で幅だけを変える。**

Finally, we vary only the width in the same Gaussian, constant-positive-$K$ model. For leaky ReLU, the relative zero $r$ does not depend on the width, but the corresponding absolute position $m=rs$ becomes deeper as the width grows, because $r$ is negative. For ELU, the relative zero of the self-form itself deepens as the width grows. For SiLU and GELU, we obtain the numerical result that a zero exists only for $s<0.7254$ and $s<0.4249$, respectively. ReLU has no zero in a living state from the start. The width thus moves the sign boundary of the self-form, but the growth of the width itself is not derived.

### 3.4 Functions lost after sinking

**〔3.4-1〕負側への位置変化によって失われる機能は、活性化で異なる。**

The function lost by a shift of the position toward the negative side differs across activations. ReLU has zero response on closed inputs; ELU, SiLU, and GELU have a small absolute derivative deep on the negative side. Leaky ReLU keeps a constant slope, yet when most inputs fall on the same branch, the unit approaches a linear map over that range (one-sidedness). All of these occur separately from death, in which the unit is closed on every input. How much a unit that has lost its response reduces the relearning of the whole network is not determined by the shape of the activation, so Section 4.4 measures it by exchanging only the response.

## 4 MLP: verifying the four stages of the mechanism in order

**〔4-0〕§3の四段を、一隠れ層（4.1）と二隠れ層のMLP（4.2〜4.4）で順に確かめる。**

We verify the four stages of Section 3 in order, in a one-hidden-layer MLP (Section 4.1) and in two-hidden-layer MLPs (Sections 4.2 to 4.4).

### 4.1 The push is created by learning and rebuilt at every task

**〔4.1-1〕一隠れ層MNISTの5活性化（§3.3の5つ、各2 seed）で、§3.1の押しが学習で作られ、課題ごとに作り直され、実際にユニットを沈めることを確かめた。**

In one-hidden-layer MNIST with the five activations of Section 3.3 (two seeds each), we confirmed that the push of Section 3.1 is created by learning, is rebuilt at every task, and actually sinks the units. The expected push at the switch rose as a nearly constant multiple of $\log C-L$, the amount by which the task-1 loss had fallen below chance, with no threshold-like step (quartiles of the ratio after 50% training accuracy: 0.18 to 0.22). In a post-hoc analysis over 30 tasks, $\kappa$ at the end of each task never fell below 0.83 times its value at the end of task 1, and from task 2 on, 74 to 95% of $\kappa$ was rebuilt by the change of the features. In the first 200 updates after a switch (Adam), 82 to 100% of the living units actually sank (range of the values over task intervals). Except for leaky ReLU, the self-form agreed with the expected push in direction in 90 to 100% of cases, but it carried only 13 to 60% of the magnitude (Appendix D). Returns within a task occurred inside the negative side: of 1,000 units that had once entered $p<1/2$, only 4 were back at $p\ge1/2$ at the end of a later task.

### 4.2 Blocking the pathways: centering and bias suppression

**〔4.2-1〕§3.2の二つの経路を別々に塞ぐ。二隠れ層の乱数ラベルCIFAR・raw・ReLUで、入力平均を引くC、両隠れ層の出力平均を引くH、両者を組み合わせたCHを比較した。**

We block the two pathways of Section 3.2 separately. In two-hidden-layer random-label CIFAR with raw inputs and ReLU, we compared three operations: C subtracts the input mean, H subtracts the output mean of both hidden layers, and CH combines the two. With C alone the second layer was lost, and with H alone the first layer was lost; in each case the lost layer was the one whose input-mean pathway stayed open. The online accuracy over tasks 31 to 50 was 11.33% for C and 98.67% for CH. CHB, which additionally suppresses the bias, reached 98.54%. CHB adds two operations to CH from task 1 on: the first-layer bias is zeroed after every update, and decoupled weight decay is applied to the second-layer bias.

**〔4.2-2〕別の10 seed（10–19）で200課題まで調べると、課題181–200のonline精度は、CHが62.98%、CHBが99.03%だった。**

Another 10 seeds (10 to 19) were run to 200 tasks. The online accuracy over tasks 181 to 200 was 62.98% for CH and 99.03% for CHB; CH declined in all 10 seeds, while CHB kept its high accuracy. In the first layer with centered inputs, the common position over the training images coincides with the bias ($m_1=b_1$), and under CH it sank to the negative side. As Section 3.2 states, blocking the weight pathway by centering leaves the push in the bias pathway, and long-term relearning was preserved only when that pathway was blocked as well.

### 4.3 Stopping the width: growth caps

**〔4.3-1〕§3.3では、幅が自己形の零点を深める活性化（ELU）と、生きた零点の無い活性化（ReLU）があった。**

Section 3.3 distinguished an activation whose self-form zero is deepened by the width (ELU) from one that has no zero in a living state (ReLU). We placed a cap on the growth of the weights to stop the width from growing, and asked whether this difference appears in relearning. The radius of each unit is the row norm of its input weights at the end of task 1; from task 2 on, after every Adam update, a row that exceeds its radius is projected back onto it (cap12 for both layers, cap1 and cap2 for one layer).

**〔4.3-2〕乱数ラベルCIFAR・std・ELUでは、課題31–50のonline精度が対照10.09%に対しcap12で85.98%となり、全10 seedで崩壊を防いだ。**

In random-label CIFAR with standardized inputs and ELU, the online accuracy over tasks 31 to 50 was 85.98% under cap12 against 10.09% for the control, and the collapse was prevented in all 10 seeds. Capping one layer was not enough (cap1 11.91%, cap2 25.10%). The norm of the second-layer input mean grew from 52.6 to 1157 in the control between tasks 1 and 50, and stayed at 40 under cap12 (seed average).

**〔4.3-3〕同じ乱数ラベルCIFARのReLUは、対照が床（11.3%）にあり、cap12でも変わらなかった。**

For ReLU in the same random-label CIFAR, the control was at the floor (11.3%), and cap12 did not change it. For ReLU in real-label 5+1 CIFAR, cap12 lowered the late online accuracy by 3.6 points [97.5% confidence interval $-6.7$ to $-0.5$]; the second-layer position became shallower, but the death rate did not fall (Appendix D). Up to this point the results agree with the reading of Section 3.3: ELU, whose zero deepens with the width, is rescued when the width is stopped, and ReLU, which has no zero in a living state, does not reopen when the width is stopped. The exception is ELU in real-label 5+1, where cap12 was worse by 2.0 points. The registered prediction that the effect splits by activation alone fails here, and the cost of the cap to learning in a fresh network (0.06 points) cannot explain it (Appendix D).

### 4.4 Exchanging the response: the effect on relearning

**〔4.4-1〕§3.4の応答の喪失が学び直しを削るかを、分岐時の重み・活動・予測を保ったまま第2層の応答だけを入れ替えて調べる。**

We test whether the loss of response of Section 3.4 reduces relearning by exchanging only the second-layer response, while keeping the weights, the activities, and the predictions at the branch point. Let $z_i^0(x)$ be the preactivation at the branch point and $z_i^{\rm src}(x)$ the preactivation of the source network. We fix their difference $o_i(x)=z_i^{\rm src}(x)-z_i^0(x)$ and set the output after the branch to $\varphi(z_i^0)+\varphi(z_i+o_i)-\varphi(z_i^0+o_i)$. At the branch point, the activities, the logits, the loss, and the weights coincide with the control, and only the derivative used in training becomes $\varphi'(z_i^{\rm src})$. After the branch, learning of the weights and the features resumes, and the derivative moves with the preactivation as $\varphi'(z_i+o_i)$.

**〔4.4-2〕同じ軌道の早期と劣化後で応答を双方向に交換した（表）。**

We exchanged the response in both directions between the early and the degraded states of the same trajectory (Table 1). The restoring direction inherits the Adam history; the direction that hands a low response to the early network resets the history in both the control and the intervention. In all three settings, restoration raised the relearning of the next task and the reverse exchange lowered it, and all 10 seeds agreed in direction in every comparison.

**〔4.4-3〕成長制限が保った応答の寄与も、乱数ラベルMNISTのELUで調べた。**

We also examined the contribution of the response that the growth cap preserves, in random-label MNIST with ELU. In this setting, cap12 constrains the norms of the two components of the first layer, split along the input-mean direction and its orthogonal complement, together with the row norms of the second layer; it improved on the control in all 10 seeds (online accuracy over tasks 51 to 100 about 84%, seed average). At the end of task 10, we exchanged the second-layer response between the cap12 network and the control network, resetting the Adam history in all arms. Here the offset was not fixed but was updated to follow the natural continuation of the source network. Over the next task of 6,000 updates, handing the control's response to the cap12 network lowered the online accuracy from 80.8% to 19.7%, and handing the cap12 response to the control raised it from 16.9% to 84.6%. This is the effect of an artificial intervention that shows the contribution of the response-related function; it is not a strict mediation rate of the natural benefit of the cap.

**〔4.4-4〕応答復元の効果は、復元時の勾配ノルム比またはAdamの変位比に合わせた固定学習率では再現できず…**

The effect of restoring the response was not reproduced by a fixed learning rate matched to the gradient-norm ratio or to the Adam displacement ratio at restoration; both controls fell below restoration in all six comparisons of the three settings (Appendix E).

**〔4.4-5〕MLPでは、機構の四段がそれぞれの介入で確かめられた。**

In MLPs, each of the four stages of the mechanism was confirmed by its own intervention. Learning creates the push, the mean and bias pathways carry it, stopping the width stops the sinking of ELU, and restoring the response restores relearning. The next section verifies the same four stages in convolutional networks, where the input to a hidden layer moves with learning.

## 5 CNN: verifying the same four stages in layers whose upstream changes

In convolutional networks, the inputs to hidden layers change with learning. We trained a network with 2 convolutional layers and 2 fully connected hidden layers on random-label CIFAR (1,200 images, 30 tasks, 30,000 updates per task, seeds 10–19) and real-label 5+1 CIFAR (seeds 2000–2009). We verified the four stages in the same order as in Section 4.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 5.1 押しは最終隠れ層でも学習が作る -->
### 5.1 Learning also creates the push in the final hidden layer

In a post-hoc analysis that replayed saved states of a leaky ReLU network, the readout of the final hidden layer f2 realigned with the current task at every observed boundary (ratio of $\kappa$ to its value at the end of task 1: 1.4–7.0; 10/10). There were 0 violations of the lower bound in Section 3.1. Through f2's own weight pathway, the push at the switch was in the sinking direction in 99–100% of cases. Over the 200 replayed updates, 84–100% of f2 units sank. However, 97–100% of the first-order response of the position was carried by upstream layers. The f2 units that sank at switches recovered as a group within tasks and remained near the relative position $r\simeq-0.6$. Sinking accumulated in f1, c2, and c1 below f2. The direction of the push in the input layer c1 was an exception (Appendix H). Loading is not limited to random labels. In real-label 5+1 CIFAR, the readout also aligned with the current task in all 467 states at the ends of hard tasks. This alignment was absent before the task (cos² 0.06–0.78 versus chance 0.002; Appendix H).

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 5.2 経路：平均とbiasの両方を塞ぐ -->
### 5.2 Pathways: blocking both the mean and the bias

In a ReLU network, we added input color centering and EMA centering of all features (channel × position) from task 1 (CH′). The input-mean pathway was closed in all 4 layers. The push shifted to the bias pathway. Biases sank in all 4 layers (bias share 1.00). The layers closed (death rates at task 30: 0.77–0.88). Which layer sank depended on how the pathways were blocked. With centering of channel means alone, f1 sank through position-specific means. Under CH′, c1 and c2 sank first. We added B to CH′, projecting the biases of all 4 hidden layers onto the interval of ±0.2 times the current minibatch width after every Adam update. This raised online accuracy over tasks 21–30 from 49.5% to 94.2% (improvement in all 10 seeds). As in Section 3.2, relearning was preserved only when both pathways were blocked.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 5.3 幅：非対称なELUだけが幅とともに沈む -->
### 5.3 Width: only asymmetric ELU sinks as the width grows

In real-label 5+1 CIFAR, extending the number of updates per task from 780 to 2,340 and 7,800 increased the FC2 width in all 4 conditions: ReLU, ELU, and 2 variants of Snake (from 6.7 to 31.6 for ELU). Only ELU sank and lost response as the width grew ($r$ −0.19→−0.73; mean derivative 0.65→0.27). Its online accuracy over the first 780 updates of late hard tasks fell by 1.17 points at 7,800 updates. Snake, which matches its curvature scale to the width, maintained a mean derivative of 0.9. Under ReLU, 94% of FC2 units died. As in Section 3.3, only the activation whose sinking is deepened by width lost response. Extending the budget, however, does not manipulate width alone. In a separate run in the same setting, the natural network's FC2 width was 34 at the end of task 30. A width cap held the FC2 width at 1.4. It kept the mean derivative at 1.00. Online accuracy over the first 780 updates fell by 5.1 points (10/10). Both networks reached 100% training accuracy at task end. Test accuracy under the cap was 0.2 points higher on average (descriptive). ELU in this setting did not deteriorate in late tasks as tasks accumulated. Extending the budget slowed only the first 780 updates. There is no loss to rescue.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 5.4 応答：予防が保った応答を交換する -->
### 5.4 Response: exchanging the response preserved by prevention

Starting from the ends of task 30 under CH′ and CH′＋B, we exchanged the response fields of all 4 hidden layers using the operation in Section 4.4. Online accuracy over the first 3,000 updates of the next task rose from 14.6% to 58.2% in the CH′ recipient (10/10). It fell from 71.4% to 60.0% in the CH′＋B recipient (9/10). The improvement in the rescue direction persisted to task end (online accuracy over the last 3,000 updates: 37.4% versus 99.2%; Appendix H). In the reverse direction, the transplanted low response reopened within 100 updates. The degradation did not persist into the late part of the task. At random-label switches, CE reflects confidence on the previous task. We therefore compared relearning using online accuracy. Adding the same B after degradation reopened the response. The accuracy improvement remained inconclusive (+2.9 points; 7/10). Blocking the pathways is preventive rather than curative.

The four stages also held in convolutional networks. What changed was that upstream layers carried the position and that sinking accumulated below the final hidden layer.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ## 6 Transformer：2ブロックの文字GPTで四段を確かめる -->
## 6 Transformer: verifying the four stages in a 2-block character GPT

We trained a 2-block pre-LN character GPT (ReLU FFNs, without biases or LN $\beta$) on a sequence of random second-order Markov languages for 100 tasks (10,000 updates per task, seeds 0–9). We measure relearning in three phases. T015 is the first update at which the 50-update moving average of excess loss reaches 0.15 or below (capped at 10,000). E1000 is the average over updates 951–1,050. ELast is the average over the last 1,000 updates. The main evaluation window is tasks 91–100. Without biases, the FFN position is $m_j=w_j^\top\bar y$. Here $\bar y$, the token mean of the FFN input after LN, changes with the upstream network. We call the two FFNs the readout-adjacent FFN L1 and the FFN one step before the readout L0. LN does not stop sinking. In the 4-block setting, networks with LN also sank in all 10 seeds. They did not exhibit plasticity loss over 50 tasks, whereas the network without LN did (Appendix H). The main text uses the 2-block, 100-task setting with LN having only γ, in which learning can be followed through to plasticity loss. We interpret the delay in plasticity loss under LN as follows. Normalization restores the scale, so the harm caused by width acts only through the freezing of rotation. This requires sustained growth of row norms (Section 6.3). This interpretation points in the same direction as accounts in which norm growth under normalization reduces the effective learning rate [Lyle 2024; Kosson 2024]. This network first learned successfully and then lost plasticity in all 10 seeds. The push and response loss in L1 preceded the loss of plasticity (9/10). Negative displacements at switches concentrated in L1 (10/10).

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 6.1 押し：学習が向きを揃える -->
### 6.1 Push: learning aligns the direction

In a post-hoc analysis of saved states from 10 seeds (decision rules registered before the analysis), the head realigned with the current task in all 700 task-end states ($\kappa>0$; 0 lower-bound violations). The push to L1 at the switch was in the sinking direction for 83–89% of units at the ends of tasks 1–9 (46–67% at the ends of tasks 89–100). At initialization, the magnitude of the push was 1.2–2.1 times that at the end of task 1, yet its directions were evenly split (47–51%). Learning aligns the direction of the push, rather than its magnitude (10/10; Appendix H).

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 6.2 経路：上流の共通の書き込みが運ぶ -->
### 6.2 Pathways: a common write from upstream carries the push

Of the position change in L1 over the 100 updates immediately after a switch, 92–99% of the sinking component was carried by the movement of the upstream input mean, $w^\top\Delta\bar y$. Its source was the common write from the upstream FFN (FFN0). Fixing this common component for only the 100 updates at the switch reduced L1 sinking over tasks 2–10 by a median of 53% (a reduction of at least 40% in 9/10 seeds). There is more than one carrier. Over tasks 41–50, the common component of attention 1 acted in the lifting direction. Fixing attention 1 increased sinking (9/10). Fixing FFN0 reversed the direction to lifting (8/10).

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 6.3 幅：L0の死と回転の凍結 -->
### 6.3 Width: death in L0 and the freezing of rotation

The row norms in L0 grew from 2.4 to 12.5. By task 100, 69% of L0 units had died. Relearning collapsed. A cap that shrinks the rows of the up projections in both FFNs according to the width at the end of task 1 improved all three phases over tasks 91–100 in 10/10 seeds (median paired difference in E1000: −1.12). Even under the cap, the readout-adjacent L1 was 99% closed by task 10 and later recovered. Death in L1 is therefore not sufficient for collapse. The recovery is supported by feedback through the denominator of the final LN: blocking this feedback prevented recovery in 5 of 6 eligible seeds, and scaling the final LN's $\gamma$ by 0.3 recovered L1 within one task in 10/10 seeds (Appendix H). The harm caused by width takes the form of the freezing of rotation. The $1-\cos$ of the weights between task ends fell from 0.116 to 0.001. ELR projects only L0's row norms back to their values at the end of task 1. It multiplies the preactivations by the control row-norm ratio. ELR outperformed the control in all three phases in 10/10 seeds. It matched the cap (E1000 difference: −1.13; difference from the cap: +0.01). L0 outputs remained 1.7–3.1 times those of the control. A small output scale is therefore not required.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ### 6.4 応答：L0の応答場を双方向に換える -->
### 6.4 Response: exchanging L0's response field in both directions

Response loss under ReLU consisted entirely of death. In the 4-block setting, death in the readout-adjacent FFN (L3) and delays in reaching the threshold tracked one another task by task (correlations +0.63 and +0.86; 10/10). Starting from the end of task 90, we replaced L0's activities using the same form as in Section 4.4. The initial activities and predictions matched the natural network. Only the local derivative changed. Over tasks 91–100, E1000 improved by 0.69 when the cap network's field was transferred. It improved by 0.86 when the width was matched to the recipient. It improved by 0.48 when the thresholds of closed units were raised. Transferring the control's field to the cap network worsened E1000 by 0.015. All 4 comparisons had $p=.0078$ after Holm correction. Rescue was partial (recovery ratio 0.37–0.68). Even when 77% of L0 in the cap network was closed, the degradation was small.

The four stages also held in the 2-block character GPT. What changed was the identity of the carrier (common components of upstream layers that change with learning) and the form of the harm caused by width (the freezing of rotation). Both are part of the stated mechanism.

<!-- 日本語見出し: ## 7 kunekune：応答と曲がりの尺度を保つ設計 -->
## 7 kunekune: a design that preserves response and the curvature scale

Stages (iii) and (iv) of the mechanism suggest a design principle: response is preserved as the width grows if the distribution remains in a region where different inputs can use different slopes. Unlike prior work that organizes activations by their negative-side slopes and tail shapes [Lillo & Cheney 2026], we match the curvature scale to the preactivation width. The kunekune activation retains the curvature of Snake [Ziyin 2020] within a finite interval and joins it to straight lines outside that interval. It matches the curvature scale $\alpha_{\rm s}$, distinct from ELU's $\alpha$, to running statistics of each unit's preactivation width. Gradients do not pass through these statistics (definition in Appendix F). We call Snake with the same scaling rule adaptive Snake.

In two-hidden-layer MLPs on random-label CIFAR, kunekune achieved online accuracy over tasks 31–50 of 95.7% with raw inputs and 98.7% with standardized inputs. Adaptive Snake achieved 95.5% and 98.7%, respectively. Both exceeded leaky ReLU at 72.6% and 96.6% (medians over 10 seeds; ReLU and ELU were at the floor). In real-label 5+1 CIFAR MLPs, online accuracy on late hard tasks was 66.7% for kunekune and 67.1% for adaptive Snake, versus 42.4% for ReLU. They outperformed Shrink & Perturb [Ash & Adams 2020], CBP [Dohare 2024], and ReDo [Sokar 2023], tuned over a small set of candidates, by 12–17 points. They met the ±0.5-point equivalence criterion with L2 Init [Kumar 2025] tuned for this task ($\lambda=10^{-3}$). This comparison used 20 seeds, including an additional replication. ReDo reduced the second-layer death rate from 33.6% to 0.2%. It closed only 43% of the gap from ReLU to adaptive Snake. Tracking the scale is necessary. Fixing $\alpha_{\rm s}$ at its initial value lowered late accuracy by 4.6–6.1 points. Fixed-scale Snake ranked only 12th and 16th among 19 conditions despite its high mean response. High mean response alone does not explain performance. In contrast, the condition with the scale fixed at the end of task 1 remained among the top performers. For these 30 tasks, adaptation to the initial width was sufficient. Among 12 real-label CNN conditions with the learning rate and shape calibrated separately for each condition, ELU ($\alpha=3.6$, outside the sign conditions of Section 3.3) ranked first in online accuracy. Snake with the scale fixed at the end of task 1 beat 7 conditions other than ELU. It ranked first in test accuracy. ReLU with ReDo or L2 Init remained far behind. This first-place result was obtained with 780 updates per task. When the budget was extended to 7,800 updates, ELU lost response as the width grew, as described in Section 5.3. Kunekune outperformed ELU in online accuracy over the first 780 updates (paired difference +0.7 points [95% interval +0.2–+1.3; 8/10]). Its test accuracy was also higher by +2.0 points [+1.4–+2.5; 10/10]. These are post-hoc descriptions from saved results, not a registered comparison. Appendix F provides a table of late online accuracy and intervals for all conditions in both settings.

## 8 Discussion

**Common links.** A single mechanism held across three architectures. Learning creates the push at the switch. The input mean, bias, and changing upstream layers carry it to the position. Asymmetric activations and width growth deepen the sinking. Units that have sunk lose their response. This reduces relearning. We verified each link through interventions or analyses of saved states. Blocking the pathways preserved relearning (200 tasks in MLPs and 30 tasks in CNNs). Fixing the pathways reduced sinking at early switches (FFN0 in GPT; the direction changes in late tasks). Limiting the width prevented collapse (caps for random-label ELU and GPT). Preserving rotation restored relearning (ELR in GPT). Restoring the response restored relearning (three MLP settings, all 4 CNN layers, and partially in GPT's L0). In some settings, preserving width and response instead slowed initial adaptation (ELU in real-label CNNs). The aspects that changed across architectures were the identity of the carrier (from a fixed input mean to common upstream components that change with learning) and the form of the harm caused by width (from a deeper position to the freezing of rotation). Both are part of the stated mechanism. In deep networks, the readout-adjacent layer recovers while its upstream continues to sink (f2 and f1 in CNNs, L1 and L0 in GPT). Our interpretation is that weights that grew during sinking do not recover through reopening alone. They recover if growth is constrained or rotation is preserved. The design in Section 7 uses the activation shape to act preventively on the width and response stages of this mechanism.

**Scope.** We distinguish prevention from recovery, online accuracy from CE, speed of attainment from level, and death on a probe (a fixed evaluation set) from strict death. We do not claim monotonic sinking in all layers or irreversibility. The 4-block networks with LN sank without losing plasticity over 50 tasks. LN did not stop sinking. Evaluation covered 200 tasks in random-label MLPs, 30 tasks in CNNs, 100 tasks in GPT, and the single task after an exchange. The MLP centering, cap, and exchange experiments, the CNN B and exchange experiments, and the GPT cap experiment were registered runs. The GPT pathway, response, and ELR interventions were post-hoc runs started from saved states. Their decision rules and predictions were fixed before the runs. The GPT numbers apply to 2 blocks, synthetic Markov languages, and this Adam configuration and learning rate. CNN loading was analyzed post hoc. GPT $\kappa$ was analyzed post hoc on saved states from 10 seeds. In late tasks, the fraction of the push in the sinking direction fell to 46–67%. We do not report the fraction of the benefit of natural interventions mediated by response or width alone.

**Open questions.** Open questions remain about the fraction of the benefit of natural interventions carried by response, the derivation of width growth itself, the separation of rotation, response, and scale in GPT, and the trigger for natural revival. Direct response-field exchange in real-label CNNs is a future extension.
